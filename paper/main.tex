\begin{abstract}
We study how spoken Russian scientific language --- chemistry, mathematics
and physics --- can be compiled into a typed structure, and when that
compilation should refuse. The artefact is a deterministic text compiler:
a transcript goes in, and either one typed abstract syntax tree (rendered as
Unicode, \LaTeX{} or OMML) or the original words come out. Its central
promise is safety: ordinary speech must not acquire notation nobody
dictated, and a substance name or an amount inside a sentence is a mention,
not a formula. On a pinned benchmark of four hand-written corpora the
compiler rewrites \PooledRewrites{} of \PooledOrdinary{} ordinary sentences
(one-sided 95\% upper bound \PooledUpper). The grammar the hand-written
parser implements is recovered as an explicit EBNF object and checked against
an independent Earley recogniser. Every number here is text in, tree out:
no live speaker has been recorded yet, and we state precisely what that does
and does not license.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Speech recognisers transcribe words; a person dictating a lab report means
formulas, units and equations as well. Inverse text normalisation (ITN)
already converts spoken forms such as numbers and measures into written ones
\cite{zhang2019neural,zhang2021nemo}, and recent work converts spoken
mathematics into \LaTeX{} \cite{korzh2026speech2latex,hyeon2025mathspeech}.
The failure that matters most in this setting is not a missed formula but an
invented one: a sentence \emph{about} sulphuric acid turned into
H$_2$SO$_4$, or the conjunction \ru{«и»} read as the letter $i$. Neural
normalisers are known to make such unrecoverable errors
\cite{sproat2017rnn,zhang2019neural}; grammars are the usual guard.

This work makes three contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A refusal-first compiler whose policy for ordinary prose is stated as
        rules with tests on both sides: what must stay words and what must
        still compile (Section~\ref{sec:policy}).
  \item The grammar of the hand-written parser written down as an EBNF object,
        its LL(1) conflicts with concrete phrases, and an Earley reference
        compared with the parser on every corpus (Section~\ref{sec:grammar}).
  \item A measurement discipline: pinned corpora, pooled admission gates and
        explicit bounds instead of point estimates (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Problem statement}
Let $a$ be an utterance (audio), $w$ a transcript and $t$ a typed tree. A
noisy-channel reading chooses
\[
  \hat t = \arg\max_{t} \; P(t)\,P(w \mid t),
\]
with $P(t)$ from a grammar and $P(w \mid t)$ a model of recognition errors.
Our compiler does not yet score; it decides. Following the reject option of
Chow \cite{chow1970optimum} and selective prediction
\cite{elyaniv2010foundations,geifman2017selective}, every reading carries a
parse level $c \in \{1.0, 0.95, 0.7, 0.0\}$ and a span inside a sentence is
inserted only at $c \ge 0.9$. The level is an engineering grade of the parse,
not a probability; turning it into a calibrated one is an open question of
the programme.

\section{The compiler}
The pipeline is
\begin{center}
transcript $\to$ candidate readings $\to$ typed AST $\to$ checks $\to$
Unicode / \LaTeX{} / OMML,
\end{center}
or the original words. The core library has no audio dependency. Two entry
points are measured: \texttt{interpret} reads the whole string as one
construct or refuses; \texttt{interpret\_utterance} keeps ordinary words and
replaces only proven spans. Renderers never re-parse the transcript.

\section{Prose stays words}\label{sec:policy}
The policy was decided case by case on traps found in the project's own text
and in coursework written by a chemist for another purpose:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a substance name inside a sentence is mentioned, not dictated
        (\ru{«серная кислота хранится в лаборатории»} stays words);
  \item so is an amount, a number with a unit
        (\ru{«на каждый моль хлора расходуется один моль водорода»});
  \item a conjunction between two operands is a word, not the letter $i$
        (\ru{«икс и игрек»} was $xiy$);
  \item a dictated list compiles, prose with a verb does not.
\end{itemize}
Each rule is pinned by tests that try to pass it the wrong way: the
forbidden rewrite fails, and the legitimate dictation (\ru{«два моля»},
\ru{«запиши …»}, a reaction with \ru{«стрелка»}) still compiles.

\section{The grammar as an object}\label{sec:grammar}
The recursive-descent parser is described by an EBNF grammar written from
the code, each rule referenced to the function that implements it. Its
FIRST/FOLLOW sets expose LL(1) conflicts; boundary commands
(\ru{«начало дроби … конец дроби»}) remove some of them, natural speech
keeps the rest. An Earley recogniser \cite{earley1970efficient} over the
same EBNF serves as an independent reference. Over all four corpora in both
modes, both accept \DiffBothAccept{} and both reject \DiffBothReject{}
token sequences; the grammar alone accepts \DiffEarleyOnly{} (spelled
chemistry the context-free rules cannot exclude) and the parser alone
accepts \DiffParserOnly. A further \DiffTokenizeFail{} inputs never reach
comparison because the mathematics tokenizer refuses ordinary words.

\section{Limitations}
No live speaker has been recorded; word error rate is unmeasured; every
benchmark record is hand-written text. The parse level is not calibrated.
The chemistry grammar is not yet covered by the Earley reference, and no
grammar probabilities have been estimated.

\section{Reproducibility}
Every table in this paper is generated from the reports in
\texttt{research/results}; a test fails when the paper and the reports
disagree. The corpora, their manifests and the commands that regenerate the
